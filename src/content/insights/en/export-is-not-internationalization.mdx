Most companies that say they want to "enter the U.S." mean they want to sell products in the U.S. That is export. It is a legitimate business, and for many products it is the right first step. But it is not internationalization, and confusing the two is the single most common reason expansions into the Americas stall.

## Two different projects

Export looks for orders. It is short-term and transactional, product-focused and price-driven, usually built around one distributor, and reactive by nature: you respond to demand.

Internationalization looks for a position in the market. It is long-term and relationship-driven, market-focused and value-driven, built around the entire ecosystem — retailers, distributors, buyers, institutions, media — and proactive: you create opportunities before they officially exist.

The goal of export is to sell today. The goal of internationalization is to be commercially relevant for years.

## What it actually requires

Becoming part of a market is built on nine capabilities: local presence, relationships, a local team, market intelligence, a commercial network, reputation, business development, infrastructure and continuity. None of them can be bought in a single transaction, and none of them can be managed from thousands of miles away.

That is why we tell companies to make the United States a market, not an experiment. An experiment has a budget and an end date. A market has a team, a warehouse, a CRM and a calendar of visits.

## The Miami advantage

Miami is where the three continents that matter to most international companies meet. PortMiami and MIA make it the logistics gateway of the Americas; Brickell concentrates international capital; a bonded warehouse here lets goods wait for the U.S. market without paying duty, and leave for Latin America without ever paying it.

An international company that starts from Miami with a team already operating, a warehouse already open and relationships already built is not exporting. It is internationalizing — from day one.
